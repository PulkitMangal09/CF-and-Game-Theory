1Growing money forward (compounding)

The idea. When you keep money in a bank, it earns interest every year. Next year you earn interest on that interest too. This is called compounding. Each year your money gets multiplied by \((1+r)\).

Main formula
\[ FV = PV \times (1+r)^t \]
LetterMeaning in simple words
\(PV\)Money you put in today (present value)
\(FV\)Money you will have later (future value)
\(r\)Interest rate per year, written as a decimal (10% = 0.10)
\(t\)Number of years

One formula, three types of question. Find out which letter is missing:

  • Missing FV (“what will it grow to?”): put the numbers in and calculate.
  • Missing r (“what is the rate?”, “annualized return?”):
    Step 1: Growth \(= FV \div PV\).   Step 2: Take the \(t\)-th root, which is \(\text{growth}^{1/t}\).   Step 3: Subtract 1.
  • Missing t (“how many years?”): \( t = \log(FV \div PV) \div \log(1+r) \). You can use log or ln; just use the same one on the top and the bottom.
Mini example: Rs 100 at 10% for 2 years becomes \(100 \times 1.1 \times 1.1 = 121\), not 120. The extra Re 1 is interest earned on interest.
Trap: Never divide the total growth by the number of years. “Triples in 10 years” is not 200% ÷ 10 = 20% per year.

\[ r = \left(\frac{FV}{PV}\right)^{1/t} - 1 \]

1BFind the number of years

\[ t = \frac{\log(FV/PV)}{\log(1+r)} \qquad \text{(for doubling, } FV/PV = 2\text{)} \]

Quick check for doubling (Rule of 72): years ≈ 72 ÷ rate in %. At 8%, that gives 72 ÷ 8 = 9 years.

1CInterest added more than once a year, or continuously

This is the same main formula. If interest is added \(n\) times a year, split the rate into \(n\) pieces (\(r/n\)) and use \(n\) times as many periods (\(n \times t\)). If interest is added every single moment (“continuously compounded”), use \(e\).

\[ FV = PV \times \left(1+\frac{r}{n}\right)^{n\,t} \qquad\qquad \text{Continuous: } FV = PV \times e^{\,r\,t} \]
\(n\)How many times a year interest is added: 1 = yearly, 2 = half-yearly, 4 = quarterly, 12 = monthly, 365 = daily
\(e\)A fixed number, about 2.718. Use the \(e^x\) key on the calculator.
Rule: for the same rate, adding interest more often gives slightly more money. Continuous compounding gives the most.

2Present value (PV) and everything built on it

The idea. Rs 100 next year is worth less than Rs 100 today. Money you have today can go into the bank and grow. Present value answers one question: “How much is this future money worth today?”

It is Family 1 run backwards. There we multiplied by \((1+r)^t\). Here we divide by it. This is called discounting.

Main formula
\[ PV = \frac{FV}{(1+r)^t} \]
\(FV\)Money you will receive in the future
\(r\)Interest rate. The question may call it the discount rate, cost of capital, required rate of return or prevailing interest rate. They all mean the same thing here.
\(t\)How many years away the money is

Every tool in this family is built from PV:

ToolWhat it is, in simple wordsGood project if…
PV of many paymentsFind the PV of each payment, then add them—
NPV(PV of all money coming in) minus (cost today)NPV > 0
PI(PV of all money coming in) divided by (cost today)PI > 1
IRRThe interest rate that makes NPV exactly 0IRR > required rate
PaybackYears until the money received adds up to the cost (no PV used)Shorter is better

Bond prices (Family 5) and perpetuity, annuity and EMI (Family 3) are also PV, worked out with shortcuts.

2APV of several payments

\[ PV = \frac{C_1}{(1+r)^{1}} + \frac{C_2}{(1+r)^{2}} + \dots + \frac{C_t}{(1+r)^{t}} \]

Each payment is divided by \((1+r)\) raised to its own year.

2BNet Present Value (NPV)

\[ NPV = \underbrace{\frac{C_1}{(1+r)} + \frac{C_2}{(1+r)^2} + \dots}_{\text{PV of money coming in}} \;-\; \text{Cost today} \]
Rule: NPV more than 0 means the project is worth doing. If you can pick only one project, pick the one with the highest NPV.
Q252026 T1MCQ · 4 marks

You are looking to invest in one new project, and you have three options: Project-A is expected to generate Rs 10 lakh after two years and Rs 20 lakh after 5 years; Project-B is expected to generate Rs 6 lakh each year for 6 years (beginning next year); Project-C is expected to generate Rs 40 lakh after 6 years. Each one of the projects costs Rs 25 lakh today. The required rate of return on these projects is 10%. Which one of the projects should you choose according to NPV criterion?

  1. Project-A
  2. Project-B
  3. Project-C
  4. All three projects have the same NPV

  3. C: \( 40 \div 1.1^6 = 22.58 \). NPV \(= 22.58 - 25 = -2.42\)

Only B has a positive NPV. Answer: B, Project-B.

2CProfitability Index (PI)

\[ PI = \frac{\text{PV of money coming in}}{\text{Cost today}} \]
Rule: PI more than 1 means you get back more value than you put in, so the project is good. PI > 1 is the same as NPV > 0.

2DInternal Rate of Return (IRR)

\[ \text{IRR is the } r \text{ for which } \; \frac{C_1}{(1+r)} + \frac{C_2}{(1+r)^2} + \dots = \text{Cost today} \quad (\text{so } NPV = 0) \]
  • Only one payment later? Then it is just Family 1: \( IRR = (\text{Payoff} \div \text{Cost})^{1/t} - 1 \).
  • Several payments? Try each option as \(r\). The one that makes the PV equal to the cost is the IRR.

3Repeating payments (shortcuts for PV and FV)

The idea. When the same payment repeats many times (rent, EMI, SIP, dividends), finding the PV of each payment one by one takes too long. These formulas are shortcuts that give the same answer as adding up every payment from Family 2.

All of them assume the first payment comes one period from now. In the papers this is written as “beginning next year” or “starting next month”.

Payment patternShortcutGives
Same amount \(C\) forever (perpetuity)\( \dfrac{C}{r} \)value today
Amount grows by \(g\) every year, forever (growing perpetuity, share price)\( \dfrac{C_1}{r-g} \)value today
Same amount \(C\) for \(T\) periods (annuity)\( C \times \dfrac{1-(1+r)^{-T}}{r} \)value today
Loan EMI: the same annuity formula, but now you know the loan and want \(C\)\( \dfrac{\text{Loan}\times r}{1-(1+r)^{-T}} \)payment per period
Saving \(C\) every period (SIP)\( C \times \dfrac{(1+r)^{T}-1}{r} \)value at the end

\(r\) and \(T\) must use the same unit. A monthly rate goes with a number of months; a daily rate goes with a number of days.

3APerpetuity (same payment forever)

\[ PV = \frac{C}{r} \]
Starting late? The formula always gives the value one year before the first payment. If the first payment comes in year 10, \(C/r\) is the value at year 9. Then bring it back to today: divide by \((1+r)^9\).

3BGrowing perpetuity & Gordon growth model (share price)

6Spending across two periods (intertemporal budget line)

The idea. You earn \(m_1\) now and \(m_2\) later. You can save (and earn interest) or borrow (and pay interest). Whatever you do, the PV of what you spend must equal the PV of what you earn.

Main formula
\[ c_1 + \frac{c_2}{1+r} = m_1 + \frac{m_2}{1+r} \]
\(m_1, m_2\)Money earned in period 1 and period 2
\(c_1, c_2\)Money spent in period 1 and period 2
\(r\)Interest rate

Three things the questions ask:

  • Slope of the budget line \(= -(1+r)\). It is always negative: spending 1 more today means giving up \((1+r)\) tomorrow.
  • Most you can spend now (spend nothing later): \( c_1 = m_1 + \dfrac{m_2}{1+r} \)
  • Most you can spend later (spend nothing now): \( c_2 = m_1(1+r) + m_2 \)

7Prices, inflation & real values

The idea. “Nominal” means measured in rupees as they are. “Real” means after taking out the effect of rising prices. Real numbers tell you how much more stuff you can actually buy.

7AReal vs nominal interest rate (Fisher equation)

\[ (1+i) = (1+r)\times(1+\pi) \]
\(i\)Nominal interest rate (what the bank quotes)
\(r\)Real interest rate (growth in what you can actually buy)
\(\pi\)(Expected) inflation rate

This is the same “multiply growth factors” idea as Family 4. Whichever one is missing, divide: \( 1 + r = (1+i) \div (1+\pi) \) or \( 1 + \pi = (1+i) \div (1+r) \). The quick estimate \( r \approx i - \pi \) is close but is not what the answer keys use.

7BCPI & inflation rate

\[ CPI_{\text{year}} = \frac{\text{cost of the basket at this year's prices}}{\text{cost of the same basket at base-year prices}} \times 100 \qquad\quad \text{Inflation} = \frac{CPI_{\text{this year}}}{CPI_{\text{last year}}} - 1 \]
  • Step 1: For each year, cost of basket = (quantity × price) added up over all items. The quantities stay the same every year.
  • Step 2: CPI = that year’s cost ÷ base-year cost × 100. The base year’s CPI is always 100.
  • Step 3: Inflation compares the two years asked about. Read carefully: is it versus the base year, or versus last year?

7CNominal GDP, real GDP & GDP deflator

\[ \text{GDP deflator} = \frac{\text{Nominal GDP}}{\text{Real GDP}} \times 100 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \text{Real GDP} = \frac{\text{Nominal GDP}}{\text{Deflator}} \times 100 \]
Nominal GDPThis year’s output valued at this year’s prices. It rises if prices rise or if output rises.
Real GDPThis year’s output valued at base-year prices. It rises only if output (quantity) rises.
DeflatorA price index. Base year = 100. A deflator of 120 means prices are 20% higher than in the base year.
Shortcut: if nominal GDP goes up but real GDP stays the same, then only prices went up.
